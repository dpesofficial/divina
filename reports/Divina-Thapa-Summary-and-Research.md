## Part 2 — Important correction on the "low" blood results

The earlier timeline repeated the laboratory's own HIGH/LOW flags, including a note that her haemoglobin and MCV looked low. **Cross-checking against paediatric reference values changes that reading.** The lab appears to be printing adult reference ranges next to a three-year-old's results.

| Result (21 Aug) | Lab's printed range | Lab flag | Correct paediatric range | Actual reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haemoglobin 11.8 g/dL | 12–18 | LOW | WHO: anaemia below **11.0 g/dL** for 6–59 months | **Not anaemic** |
| MCV 76 fL | 77–95 | LOW | Lower limit ~**74 fL** at 1.5–4 years | **Within range** |
| Creatinine 0.5 mg/dL | 0.6–1.4 | LOW | **0.30–0.50 mg/dL** for ages 2–4 | **Normal** |

This is a known and widespread problem, not a fault unique to this lab. The Canadian Laboratory Initiative on Pediatric Reference Intervals notes that laboratories worldwide routinely report adult reference intervals alongside children's results because validated paediatric intervals are not available, and that this leads to real misinterpretation.

**What this means:** those three alarming-looking flags most likely mean nothing. She does not appear to be anaemic on this evidence. Her kidney function looks normal for her age. Worth confirming with the paediatrician rather than assuming, but do not go into the appointment worried about anaemia.

**What is genuinely abnormal:** the CRP is raised. But see section 3.3 — the number is coarser than it looks, and it cannot separate viral from bacterial on its own.

## Part 3 — What the research says, issue by issue

### 3.1 The single most useful finding: vulvovaginitis is very often mistaken for recurrent UTI

This is the one worth reading twice, because it may explain most of her file.

Vulvovaginitis is the **most common gynaecological problem in prepubertal girls**. Before puberty the vulval skin is thin, oestrogen-free, and the distance between anus, vagina and urethra is short, which makes irritation and bacterial spread easy.

The critical mechanism, from NHS Greater Glasgow & Clyde referral guidance:

> Urine passing over inflamed skin causes dysuria and frequency that mimic UTI. A urine dipstick showing leucocytes alone may be due to contamination from the skin.

In other words: **a child with an inflamed vulva will complain of burning when she wees, and her urine will show white cells, without having a urine infection at all.**

There is also a genuine two-way link. A 2014 study in *Therapeutic Advances in Urology* found vulvovaginitis is associated with an altered perineal microbiome and is hypothesised to be a major contributor to actual UTIs in prepubertal girls, by increasing colonisation around the urethra with gut bacteria.

**How this maps onto Divina's file:**
- Vulval itching and redness documented on 7 May (3-month history), 17 May, 25 June
- Urinary symptoms on 17 May, 31 May, 18 July
- **Five urine cultures, one positive** (E. coli, 17 May)
- The 31 May note records burning micturition continuing *after* a completed 7-day cefixime course — with the culture from that same day growing nothing
- Repeated "pus cells" and "trace leukocytes" on urine microscopy without culture growth

That pattern is textbook for vulval irritation causing urinary symptoms and contaminated urine samples, with possibly one genuine E. coli infection layered on top.

The Royal Children's Hospital guideline adds a specific pointer:

> Children with recurrent urinary tract infection symptoms but negative cultures should be assessed for vulvodynia.

**What guidelines say to actually do about vulvovaginitis** (RCH and NHSGGC agree closely). Note how little of it is medication:

- Non-biological laundry powder; no fabric softener
- No soap, bubble bath or shampoo in the bath water; no feminine wipes
- Cotton underwear; loose clothing; nothing tight or synthetic
- Cotton nightwear rather than pyjama pants, to let air circulate
- Wipe front to back; some girls do better rinsing with water after using the toilet
- Barrier cream such as Vaseline twice daily and before swimming
- Vinegar baths — half a cup of white vinegar in a shallow bath
- **Screen for and treat constipation** — she has a documented history of functional constipation
- **Treat threadworms if present**, and treat the whole household
- Avoid antifungal creams in children still in nappies; candida is uncommon at this age

**Two things guidelines say NOT to do:**
- Never perform an internal vaginal examination or take internal vaginal swabs in a prepubescent child. External inspection only. If discharge is heavy or smelly, swab at the introitus only.
- Do not treat on leucocytes alone.

**On threadworm specifically:** she was dewormed around May 2026 and her 25 June stool test showed no ova. Worth knowing that stool examination is a poor test for threadworm — eggs are laid around the anus, not passed in stool, so the standard test is the sticky-tape test done first thing in the morning. RCH advises suspecting threadworm when a child has night-time genital or perineal itch. A negative stool result does not rule it out.

### 3.2 Imaging after a urine infection — the ultrasound that seems to be missing

NICE guideline NG224 (Urinary tract infection in under 16s, 2022):

- A child with a **lower UTI** should have an ultrasound of the urinary tract within 6 weeks **if they are under 6 months old or have had recurrent infections**
- A child **under 3 years** with **atypical and/or recurrent UTI** should have a **DMSA scan 4–6 months after** the acute infection, to look for kidney scarring
- Ultrasound during the acute infection if the UTI is atypical
- Do not routinely image for reflux after a single UTI

"Recurrent UTI" is commonly defined as **two or more episodes in six months, or three or more in twelve months**. Some paediatric definitions instead count upper versus lower tract episodes.

**How this applies:** Dr Khatri advised a USG of abdomen and pelvis on 31 May 2026, immediately after the confirmed E. coli infection. That advice lines up exactly with the guideline. **There is no ultrasound report in your papers.** If it was never done, that is the most clearly guideline-indicated test outstanding in the whole file.

Whether she formally counts as "recurrent UTI" — and therefore whether a DMSA scan is warranted — depends on how many episodes were culture-confirmed. On the evidence here, only one was. That is a specific question for the paediatrician, and the answer changes what imaging is appropriate.

### 3.3 The CRP — the number is coarser than it looks

**Correction to what I told you earlier.** I treated 96 as a precise measurement. It is not. You spotted this and you were right.

**How the number was produced.** The report prints `C-REACTIVE PROTEIN (CRP) TITER :- 96`. The word *titer* is the giveaway. This is a **latex agglutination slide test**: serum is diluted 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16 and so on, and the technician reports the last dilution at which visible clumping occurs. Concentration is then calculated as the reagent sensitivity, 6 mg/L, multiplied by that dilution.

That is exactly why her two results are 48 and 96. **6 × 8 = 48. 6 × 16 = 96.** The only values this test can ever return are 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192, 384.

Three consequences:

1. **96 does not mean 96.** It means the sample still clumped at 1:16 but not at 1:32, so the true value sits somewhere between roughly 96 and 191 mg/L.
2. **48 → 96 is a single rung** on the ladder — the smallest change the method can register. It could be a real rise, or two runs landing either side of one dilution.
3. This is not a criticism of the lab. Latex agglutination is legitimate, cheap and widely used. It is simply less precise than the immunoturbidimetric CRP a larger laboratory would run.

**What a number in that band means.** You suggested it might be compatible with viral fever. The literature supports that:

- CRP **cannot** distinguish bacterial from viral at moderate elevations of roughly **10 to 80 mg/L** — the overlap is substantial
- Research looking specifically at the **50 to 100 mg/L band** found CRP could not significantly separate bacterial from viral cases within it
- Above 100 mg/L the bacterial association strengthens, but adenovirus and several other viruses regularly push CRP past 100
- **Serial measurements beat any single value.** Falling supports the treatment working; rising suggests deterioration

**What sits alongside it in her case.** On 21 August her total white cell count was **normal at 9,400**. Lymphocytes had risen to 40% and neutrophils fallen to 55%, against 30% and 65% in July. A normal white count with a lymphocyte-predominant differential is a pattern seen more often in **viral** illness. A bacterial infection driving CRP into that range would more typically bring a raised white count with neutrophilia — which is what July looked like and August does not.

None of that is proof either way. It is a reason to want the blood culture result and a repeat CRP, rather than to assume the antibiotic is treating the right thing.

**Ask the doctor:** can a quantitative CRP be run instead of the latex slide test, and can it be repeated now to see the direction of travel?
